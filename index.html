Iron practice plan for lefties: slower swing, straighter ball

A four-stage plan for a left-handed golfer who shoots in the 90s, slices their irons, and swings too hard when it matters. It uses the range and the short-game area, and it works whether you get 20 minutes or 75.

  • Three session lengths, so a short week still counts

Written for left-handed

Everything here is mirrored for a lefty: your slice curves right to left, and your lead hand is your right. If you ever pass this to a right-handed friend, they should use the right-handed version.

Slower is only part of the fix

A slice means the clubface is open relative to your swing path. Tempo mostly fixes the path and the contact. Grip, aim, and face control fix the rest, which is why the plan has four stages instead of one tempo drill.

Check your aim

Many slicers set up with feet and shoulders pointing right of the target to compensate. Squaring up can feel like aiming left. It isn’t.

Equipment is unchecked

Shaft flex, shaft length, lie angle, and grip size can all feed a slice. If you finish Stage 2 and the curve hasn’t improved, a fitting check is worth the hour.

One pro session would speed this up

A launch monitor shows face angle and path in numbers, so you’d know which one is the bigger culprit. The plan works without it. It just won’t be targeted.

No calendar on purpose

You said your practice time varies a lot, so each stage ends with a result you need to hit rather than a week number. Plan on roughly three to five sessions per stage. That’s an estimate, not a promise. If you take two or more weeks off, repeat the first drill of your current stage before moving on.

Range quirks

Mats hide fat shots, and range balls fly shorter than course balls. Judge your shots by start line and curve, not distance. Hit off real turf when you can.

Body check

Warm up for five minutes before you swing. If you have back, hip, or shoulder issues, keep the half-swing drills and stop anything that hurts.

Not covered

Driver, putting, and course strategy. This is irons plus the pitch shots in your short-game area. A driver slice often shares the same causes, so expect some carry-over.

Why slowing down helps

When you swing hard, your upper body tends to fire first. The club gets pulled from outside the target line across the ball. That’s an out-to-in path, and for a lefty it starts the ball right. Your grip tightens at the same time, so the face stays open and the ball curves left.

A calmer swing gives your lower body time to lead, lets the club drop in front of you, and gives your hands time to square the face. Centered contact also matters more than raw speed. A smooth, well-struck iron usually goes nearly as far as a hard, off-center one.

The goal isn’t slow motion. It’s the fastest swing you can still strike well, which is usually 70 to 80 percent effort.

10Everything you’ve got. Leave this for emergencies.
7Default for practice and for the course. If contact falls apart, drop to 6.

The four stages

Work on one stage at a time. Hit the “move on when” target on two separate sessions before you advance, so a lucky day doesn’t promote you. Rotate your clubs: a 9-iron, a 7-iron, and a 5-iron are plenty.

1

Grip, aim, and slow-motion basics

Remove the setup mistakes that build the slice in before you swing, and learn what a 70 percent effort swing feels like.

Grip check
Rotate both hands slightly away from the target until you see two to three knuckles on your lead (right) hand. The “V” shapes between thumb and forefinger should point toward your left shoulder. Grip pressure should be about 4 out of 10, like holding a tube of toothpaste without squeezing any out.
Alignment sticks
Lay one stick on the ground pointing at your target, and a second along your toe line parallel to it. Check that your feet, hips, and shoulders are parallel to the target line, not pointing right of it. Take a phone photo from behind for the first few sessions.
9-to-3 half swings
With an 8-iron, swing so your lead arm goes from parallel to the ground on the backswing to parallel on the follow-through. Effort is a 3 or 4. Hit 10 balls, focusing only on center-face contact and turning your chest through the ball.
Feet-together swings
Stand with your feet touching and make smooth swings with a 7-iron. You can’t swing hard and stay balanced this way. Do 10 practice swings, then hit 10 balls.

Move on when: 7 of 10 balls come off the center of the face at about 50 percent effort, and you can set your grip and aim from memory without leaning on the alignment sticks.

2

Tempo and path

Stop the over-the-top move and let the club approach from the inside. This is the stage that does the most to cut the slice.

3-to-1 count
Say “back, two, three” on the way to the top and “hit” on the way down. The backswing takes about three times as long as the downswing. A metronome app set around 60 to 70 beats per minute can keep you honest. Swing at effort 6 to 7.
Pause at the top
Swing to the top, hold for one full second, then start down by shifting your lead hip toward the target before your shoulders move. This kills the urge to rush the transition. Hit 10 balls, then drop the pause but keep the feel.
Headcover check
Put a headcover on the ground a few inches outside the ball and slightly behind it, farther from you. If you come over the top, the club clips the headcover. Swing so it never gets touched.
Film a few swings
Record from behind and from the front at normal speed. In transition, check whether your shoulders and hands move out toward the ball first (bad) or your lower body leads (good).

Move on when: 7 of 10 full swings at effort 7 are solid, and the ball no longer starts right of your target. Starting on line or slightly left is the sign that your path has improved.

3

Face control

Even with a better path, an open face still leaks the ball right. Learn to square it at impact and shape a small, controlled curve.

Pump drill
Swing to the top, then “pump” the club down to the point where the shaft is parallel to the ground, three times. At that point the toe of the club should point roughly up, not have the face staring at the sky. Then make a full swing at effort 6.
Impact spray or tape
Put foot spray or impact tape on the face for a handful of shots. Heel strikes mean you’re too close or reaching toward the ball. Toe strikes mean you’re too far away or falling forward.
Intermediate target
Pick a spot (a divot, a leaf, a tee) about three feet in front of the ball on your target line. Aim the clubface at that spot first, then build your stance around it. It’s much easier to aim at something close.
Start it left, bring it back
On purpose, aim to start the ball a few yards left of the target and let it curve back. Hit five each with the 9-iron, 7-iron, and 5-iron. A gentle draw here is a win.

Move on when: at least 6 of 10 shots finish inside a 20-yard-wide corridor around your target, and the typical curve is under about 10 yards.

4

Transfer to the course

Make the new swing hold up when the club, the target, and the pressure keep changing.

Random practice
Change clubs and targets after every ball, and go through your full pre-shot routine each time. It feels harder than hitting 20 in a row, and it matches the course far better.
Short-game tempo
In the short-game area, hit 30 to 50 yard pitches with the same 3-to-1 count. Tempo you learn here carries into your full swing.
The ladder
Pick a 20-yard corridor. Hit three in a row inside it with your 7-iron at effort 7. Then move to the 6-iron, then the 5-iron. A miss sends you back to the start of that club.
Play nine at the range
Imagine a nine-hole course. One ball per shot, choose a club and target each time, and track which shots would have found the fairway or green.

Keep going when: you’re on the course. Stay at effort 7, keep the 3-to-1 count in your routine, and return to Stage 2 or 3 drills for a few minutes whenever the slice creeps back.

Session tracker

After each session, write down three numbers for your main club, each out of 10. Over a few sessions this shows your progress better than how you feel.

  • Contact: how many came off the center of the face.
  • Start line: how many started on or left of the target line.
  • Corridor: how many finished inside your 20-yard corridor.
